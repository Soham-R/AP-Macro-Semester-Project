Background

Understanding stock markets, price discovery, and the rise of algorithmic trading before AI.

What Are Stock Markets?

Stock markets are exchanges where buyers and sellers trade ownership shares in publicly listed companies. The two most important indices for this research are the S&P 500 - tracking the 500 largest U.S. companies - and the NASDAQ Composite, which is heavily weighted toward technology firms.

Prices are determined by supply and demand: when more investors want to buy a stock than sell it, prices rise. When confidence falls and selling pressure increases, prices decline. These movements aggregate into broader index values that economists use to gauge market health.

S&P 500

An index tracking the 500 largest publicly traded U.S. companies by market capitalization.

NASDAQ

A stock exchange and index heavily weighted toward technology and growth companies.

Market Cap

Total market value of a company’s outstanding shares. Used to weight index composition.

Liquidity

How easily an asset can be bought or sold without affecting its price.

Supply & Demand in Markets

Classical economics explains stock prices through supply and demand curves. High investor confidence and strong earnings expectations shift the demand curve right, raising equilibrium price. Panic, poor earnings, or macroeconomic shocks shift demand left, pushing prices down.

AI has disrupted this by allowing institutions to react to signals in milliseconds - compressing what once took hours into fractions of a second.

The Rise of Algorithmic Trading

Prior to the 1990s, virtually all stock trading was executed manually by human brokers on exchange floors. The introduction of electronic trading systems in the late 1990s began a decades-long shift toward automation. By the 2010s, high-frequency trading firms were executing millions of trades per second using rule-based algorithms.

Today, machine learning has taken this further. Instead of rule-based systems, AI models learn from historical market data, news sentiment, economic indicators, and even social media to identify patterns and execute trades with minimal human oversight.

70-80% Share of U.S. equity volume driven by algorithmic trading
~Microseconds Typical execution speed of high-frequency AI trades
1990s Decade when electronic trading began replacing floor brokers

The Business Cycle & AI Investment

The business cycle describes recurring phases of economic expansion, peak, contraction, and trough. AI investment has closely tracked this cycle - and in some ways amplified it.

Expansion

Investor optimism is strong, funding rises, and AI equity valuations expand quickly.

Why AI Companies Now Dominate Market Indices

The S&P 500 and NASDAQ are market-cap weighted, meaning the largest companies make up a disproportionate share of the index. As AI companies like NVIDIA, Microsoft, Apple, Alphabet, and Meta have grown to multi-trillion-dollar valuations, their price movements now drive overall index performance.

  • Market-cap weighting concentrates exposure in the largest AI firms.
  • Explosive AI revenue growth supports high valuations.
  • Institutional investors and ETFs create structural buying pressure.
  • Retail investor attention and media coverage amplify momentum.

Key Takeaways

  • Stock markets price assets through supply and demand, now heavily accelerated by AI algorithms.
  • Algorithmic trading accounts for the majority of equity volume.
  • AI investment behavior tracks and can amplify the business cycle.
  • Market-cap weighting means AI company performance can dominate index returns.
